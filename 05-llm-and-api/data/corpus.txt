# Corpus: the body text of the READMEs of Chapters 1–4 of this repo (tables, code, links and references removed), used for the labs in Chapter 5.
Chapter 1 — Where Machine Learning Came From
On July 8, 1958, The New York Times ran a story under the headline
"New Navy Device Learns By Doing".
More than sixty years later, we start this repo the same way.
This chapter is about how machines learned, step by step, to "learn." It runs from the neuron equations a runaway teenager wrote down in 1943
to a team of AI agents splitting up the work of writing code in 2026. Along the way there are geniuses, gambles and winters, and one person who changed history with two gaming graphics cards in his bedroom.
How to read this chapter:
1. Read the stories first (Acts 1–12). Every name, date, place and quote has a source, listed in the References at the end.
For anecdotes that rest on a single source, I say so: "according to X's recollection" or "as X reported."
2. Then run the three small labs and recreate three key moments, from 1958, 1969 and 1986, yourself.
3. Save the Concepts for last. Every concept planted in the stories is explained in full there.
Prologue — Jul 1958, Washington: A Machine That "Learns By Doing"
On July 7, 1958, the US Office of Naval Research held a press conference at the US Weather Bureau in Washington.
The star of the show was a 29-year-old psychologist from the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, Frank Rosenblatt.
His prop was an IBM 704 computer that weighed 5 tons and filled a whole room.
The demo sounded simple: show the machine one punched card after another, some marked on the left, some on the right.
Nobody wrote rules telling it what "left" and "right" meant. After 50 cards, it had learned on its own to tell left from right.
The next day, the headline in The New York Times read "New Navy Device Learns By Doing; Psychologist Shows Embryo of Computer
Designed to Read and Grow Wiser."
The article also said the Navy expected it would one day "be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence."
Rosenblatt himself called it the first machine capable of having an original idea. The New Yorker went even further:
in its view, this was the first serious rival to the human brain ever devised.
The machine was called the perceptron. To understand why it got people so excited, and why it later fell so hard,
we first have to go back 15 years, to Chicago.
Look ahead: The machine "learned," but what exactly did it learn? → Models and parameters
Act 1 — 1943, Chicago: The Runaway Boy and the Logic of Neurons
Walter Pitts was born in 1923 into a poor family in Detroit.
According to his friend Jerome Lettvin's recollections (retold in a long 2015 article in Nautilus),
at age 12 Pitts ducked into the public library to escape some street bullies, and there he found Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica.
He stayed in the library for three days, read the nearly 2,000-page work from cover to cover, found several mistakes in it, and wrote to Russell about them.
Russell wrote back inviting him to come to Cambridge as a graduate student. But he was only 12.
At 15, Pitts heard that Russell was coming to lecture at the University of Chicago, so he ran away from home to Chicago.
He wasn't enrolled. He got by on odd jobs and snuck into classes, and that's where he met a medical student, Lettvin.
Lettvin introduced him to the neurophysiologist Warren McCulloch.
McCulloch had long wanted to explain the brain with logic. He was captivated by the boy's ideas, and simply invited him to live in his house in the suburbs.
In 1943, the two published a paper, A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity.
Its core idea: a neuron can be thought of as a switch. It receives signals from other neurons.
If the signals add up to more than some threshold, it "fires"; otherwise it stays silent. Connect such switches into a network, and you can compute any logical proposition.
It was the first time anyone had written down an "artificial neuron" in mathematics. But it had one flaw: the connections in the network were designed by people. It couldn't learn.
The rest of Pitts's life was a tragedy. He went to MIT, worked with Norbert Wiener, the father of cybernetics, and was seen as a genius.
In 1952, Wiener abruptly cut off McCulloch and everyone connected with him, Pitts included. Pitts was devastated. He started drinking heavily and never got his PhD. According to Nautilus, he burned his doctoral dissertation and years of notes.
He died in May 1969, at 46. McCulloch died that September.
Look ahead: Neuron = weighted sum + threshold. → Neurons and the perceptron
Act 2 — 1949–1950: A Rule for Learning, a Machine That Thinks
So now there was a neuron. But how does the brain learn things?
In 1949, the Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb proposed in his book The Organization of Behavior
that if neuron A repeatedly takes part in making neuron B fire, the connection from A to B grows stronger.
Later generations boiled it down to a catchy rhyme: "cells that fire together, wire together."
It was the first time anyone had clearly proposed that learning means changing the strength of connections.
A year later, in 1950, Alan Turing published Computing Machinery and Intelligence in the philosophy journal Mind.
Its very first sentence: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'"
He introduced the famous "imitation game," later known as the Turing test.
The last section of the paper is titled "Learning Machines," and in it Turing wrote that
instead of writing a program to simulate an adult's mind, why not write one that simulates a child's, and then educate it?
A psychologist said learning is adjusting connections; a mathematician said we might as well build a machine that learns.
It would take Rosenblatt to truly bring these two ideas together.
Look ahead: Adjusting connection strengths = adjusting parameters. → Models and parameters
Act 3 — 1956–1959: One Summer, and a Game of Checkers
In 1955, four young scholars co-signed a funding proposal:
John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky,
Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon, the father of information theory.
They proposed a two-month, ten-person workshop at Dartmouth College in the summer of 1956.
The term "artificial intelligence" first appeared in this very proposal.
The proposal was wildly optimistic: "We think that a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems
if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer." The problems included getting machines to use language, form abstract concepts, and improve themselves.
(Spoiler: one summer was nowhere near enough. Seventy years later, some of these problems are still unsolved.)
Around the same time, IBM engineer Arthur Samuel was working on something more down-to-earth: teaching a computer to play checkers.
His program remembered the positions it had played and whether they eventually led to a loss or a win. The more it played, the better it got.
On February 24, 1956, the program made its public debut on television. According to historical material compiled by the University of Alberta's checkers AI team,
IBM president Thomas Watson arranged for the program to be shown to shareholders and predicted it would push IBM's stock up 15 points. It did.
In 1959, Samuel published his paper Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers.
That's how the term "machine learning" caught on.
In 1962, his program beat Robert Nealey, a self-described checkers master.
One sentence is often cited as Samuel's definition of machine learning: "Field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without being explicitly programmed."
But you won't find that sentence in his 1959 paper. It's a later paraphrase. What the paper actually says is:
"Programming computers to learn from experience should eventually eliminate the need for much of this detailed programming effort."
Look ahead: Learning from experience, getting better the more it plays. → What is machine learning, Three ways to learn
Act 4 — 1958–1960: Rosenblatt and His "Electronic Brain"
Now back to the press conference from the Prologue.
Rosenblatt pieced the earlier ideas together: take the McCulloch–Pitts neuron (weighted sum + threshold),
add a Hebb-like rule (adjust connection strengths based on experience), and you get a machine that learns.
His learning rule fits in one sentence: if it guesses right, do nothing; if it guesses wrong, adjust the weights.
The 1958 demo actually simulated the perceptron on the IBM 704. Two years later, on June 23, 1960,
purpose-built hardware, the Mark I Perceptron, was unveiled to the public:
Its "eye" was an array of 20×20 = 400 photocells, in effect a 400-pixel retina;
Each connection's weight was stored in a potentiometer;
During learning, electric motors turned those potentiometers. Every wrong guess set a motor turning.
Today the Mark I Perceptron is on display at the National Museum of American History (part of the Smithsonian Institution).
Rosenblatt was a man of many talents: he played the piano well and even helped build an observatory.
On July 11, 1971, he died in a boating accident on Chesapeake Bay. It was his 43rd birthday.
Try it: run perceptron_1958.py and recreate the demo yourself:
20×20 cards, 50 trials, learning to tell left from right. Then show it 1,000 more cards and see what happens.
Look ahead: Getting every training card right doesn't mean getting every new card right. → Generalization and overfitting
Act 5 — 1969–1973: A Book, a Wall, a Winter
Marvin Minsky, one of the organizers of the Dartmouth conference, had known Rosenblatt for a long time:
both went to the Bronx High School of Science in New York, with Minsky one year behind Rosenblatt.
The two held opposite views on neural networks and debated them publicly at academic conferences, but in private they reportedly stayed on friendly terms.
In 1969, Minsky and the MIT mathematician Seymour Papert published Perceptrons.
The book proved with rigorous math that a single-layer perceptron can do very little.
The most famous example is "exclusive or" (XOR): output 1 when the two inputs are "different," 0 when they're "the same."
A rule this simple, and the perceptron can never learn it.
The book's impact went far beyond the math itself. Funding and enthusiasm for neural network research drained away, and many researchers moved on to other things.
Two years later, Rosenblatt died in an accident, and the field lost its most powerful champion.
Winter didn't fall on neural networks alone. In 1973, the British mathematician James Lighthill,
commissioned by the UK's Science Research Council, wrote a report assessing artificial intelligence research. His verdict was harsh:
"In no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised."
The British government went on to cut AI research funding at most of its universities. That June, the BBC also recorded a televised debate at the Royal Institution:
Lighthill on one side, and three AI researchers, including John McCarthy, on the other.
This period came to be known as the first AI winter.
Try it: run xor_1969.py. The same perceptron learns AND and OR quickly,
but on XOR it never stops. You'll also see a little chart showing why no straight line can separate XOR.
Look ahead: Can one straight line separate them? → Linear separability and XOR
Act 6 — 1986: Sending the Error Backward
People had actually long known the answer to the problem: add another layer. Put a "hidden layer" between the input and the output, and XOR can be solved.
The real puzzle was: how do you adjust the hidden layer's weights? When the output is wrong, which hidden neuron is to blame?
In 1986, David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and
Ronald Williams published a paper just 4 pages long in Nature:
Learning representations by back-propagating errors. The method is called backpropagation:
first compute the error at the output, then use the chain rule from calculus to pass the error backward, layer by layer,
working out how much "blame" each weight should take, and then nudge each one a little.
To be fair, they weren't the first to invent the math.
The Finnish student Seppo Linnainmaa described a related automatic differentiation method in his 1970 master's thesis,
and the American researcher Paul Werbos proposed using it to train neural networks in his 1974 PhD thesis.
But the 1986 paper showed clearly that the hidden layer learns useful intermediate features on its own. With that, neural networks came back to life.
Hinton's own story is a fascinating one. He is the great-great-grandson of the logician George Boole:
the "Boolean logic" every computer uses today is named after his great-great-grandfather.
In 1987, he left the US for the University of Toronto in Canada. One reason: most American AI research at the time was funded by the Department of Defense, and he didn't want his research used for war.
He and his students then spent decades in Canada sticking with unfashionable neural networks, until they changed the world.
In 2018, he shared the Turing Award with Yoshua Bengio and Yann LeCun; in 2024, he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield.
Try it: run backprop_1986.py. It writes backpropagation by hand with nothing but numpy.
Watch the error come down bit by bit, then open the black box and see which intermediate concepts the hidden layer invented on its own.
Look ahead: The error flows backward, and each weight takes a small step downhill. → Loss functions and gradient descent, Backpropagation
Act 7 — 1989–1997: The Network That Read Checks, and the Champion That Couldn't Learn
In 1988, a young Frenchman, Yann LeCun, joined Bell Labs in the US.
In 1989, he used backpropagation to train a special kind of neural network to recognize handwritten ZIP codes from the US Postal Service.
Its design was inspired by the visual cortex: instead of looking at the whole image at once, it slides small "windows" across the image,
looking for local patterns like edges and corners. This is the convolutional neural network (CNN).
The technology was later used to read bank checks. According to the ACM's Turing Award announcement, a check-reading system LeCun helped develop was widely deployed by NCR and other companies,
and in the late 1990s and early 2000s, it read more than 10% of all checks in the US.
But in academia, neural networks soon fell out of favor again. In 1995, Corinna Cortes and Vladimir Vapnik published the support vector machine (SVM).
It had elegant mathematical theory behind it, and at the data sizes of the time, its results were also more reliable. Throughout the 2000s, "statistical learning" was the mainstream.
As LeCun, Hinton and others later recalled, neural network papers in those years were often rejected by conferences. This is sometimes called neural networks' "second cold spell."
Around the same time, another "AI beats human" story shook the world: in May 1997, IBM's Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov in New York.
But Deep Blue could hardly learn at all. It relied on custom chips that searched more than a hundred million positions per second, plus evaluation rules hand-written by chess experts.
It was strong, but its strength had been "written in" by people, not "learned" on its own.
Look ahead: → Convolutional neural networks
Act 8 — 2006–2009: A Dam Made of Data
In 2006, a young assistant professor, Fei-Fei Li, began turning over a question:
everyone was competing on algorithms, but what if data was the real bottleneck? Children come to understand the world because they've seen an enormous number of things.
She decided to build an image database unlike anything before it: following the structure of WordNet, a database of English words, collect upwards of a thousand photos for each of thousands of kinds of objects.
That was far too much work for students to do. In 2007, the team turned to Amazon's crowdsourcing platform, Mechanical Turk:
about 49,000 workers from 167 countries sifted through and labeled more than 160 million candidate images.
When ImageNet made its debut in 2009 at CVPR, the top computer vision conference, it had 3.2 million images,
but it was just a poster in a corner of the venue. Starting in 2010, the team ran the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC) every year:
1,000 categories and about 1.2 million training images. For the first two years, the winners all used traditional methods, and progress was slow.
Look ahead: More data, better generalization. Remember Act 2 of Lab 1? → Generalization and overfitting
Act 9 — 2012: Two Graphics Cards in a Bedroom, an Auction in a Casino
In 2012, two of Hinton's students in Toronto, Alex Krizhevsky and
Ilya Sutskever, decided to enter the ImageNet challenge.
It was Sutskever who talked Krizhevsky into it, and Krizhevsky happened to be exceptionally good at squeezing every last drop of performance out of GPUs.
Their network came to be known as AlexNet. It was too big to fit in one graphics card's memory, so they split it across two NVIDIA GTX 580 gaming cards
and trained it for five or six days. According to the Computer History Museum, the machine sat in Krizhevsky's bedroom at his parents' house.
The results stunned the field: AlexNet's top-5 error rate was 15.3%, while the runner-up's was 26.2%.
In a field used to improving by a fraction of a percentage point a year, it had jumped ahead by almost 11 points in one go. The era of deep learning had begun.
A few months later came an even more dramatic scene. Hinton and the two students had set up a three-person company, DNNresearch.
In December 2012, while the NIPS conference was being held at Lake Tahoe, he ran an auction from his room at Harrah's, the casino hotel where the conference took place.
According to Cade Metz's Genius Makers, the bidders were Google, Microsoft, Baidu and DeepMind, then still a small company,
all bidding by email. The price climbed all the way to $44 million. At that point Hinton stopped the auction and sold the company to Google.
In his view, finding the right home for the research mattered more than getting the highest price.
Look ahead: → Deep learning and GPUs
Act 10 — 2014–2016: An Idea in a Bar, Move 37 on the Board
2014, Montreal. PhD student Ian Goodfellow went to a bar called Les 3 Brasseurs
to celebrate a fellow student who had just finished a PhD. His friends were discussing a hard problem: how do you get a computer to "draw" realistic photos on its own?
Goodfellow had an idea: pit two neural networks against each other. One makes fakes, the other tells real from fake.
The forger keeps getting better at forging, and the detector keeps getting better at detecting.
According to MIT Technology Review, when he got home his girlfriend was already asleep. He coded into the early hours of the morning, then gave it a test, and it worked on the very first run.
This was the generative adversarial network (GAN).
March 2016, Seoul. DeepMind's AlphaGo faced one of the world's top players, Lee Sedol, in a five-game match.
On move 37 of Game 2, AlphaGo played a "shoulder hit" that none of the professional players could make sense of.
Lee Sedol got up and left the playing room. When he came back to the board, he stared at the move for nearly 15 minutes before he played.
European Go champion Fan Hui, who was there, said: "It's not a human move. I've never seen a human play this move." Then he added: "So beautiful."
AlphaGo itself estimated that the odds of a human player making this move were just 1 in 10,000.
In Game 4, it was Lee Sedol's turn to play a "divine move": move 78. AlphaGo likewise estimated the odds of a human playing it at just 1 in 10,000,
and its judgment then fell into confusion, and it lost the game. It was humanity's only win of the match; the final score was 4–1.
In November 2019, Lee Sedol announced his retirement. AI, he said, is "an entity that cannot be defeated."
AlphaGo was different from Deep Blue: it first learned from records of human games, then kept getting stronger by playing against itself. Nobody could have "written" move 37.
Look ahead: → Generative models and GANs, Three ways to learn (reinforcement learning)
Act 11 — 2017–2022: Eight People and One Paper
In spring 2017, eight Google researchers wrote a paper titled Attention Is All You Need.
According to a 2024 story in Wired:
The core idea, "self-attention," came first from Jakob Uszkoreit. He also came up with the name "Transformer," because he liked the sound of the word;
an early design document was even illustrated with pictures of characters from Transformers;
The paper's title is a nod to the Beatles song "All You Need Is Love";
The eight authors decided to break with the usual convention for ordering names. They put an asterisk after every name, with a footnote reading "Equal contribution. Listing order is random.";
Later, all eight left Google, to start their own companies or join other AI companies.
The Transformer threw out the old approach of reading a sentence in order, one word at a time. Instead, every word in the sentence "attends" to every other word at the same time.
This not only works better, it's also a great fit for parallel computing on GPUs, so models could finally keep getting bigger.
You may have heard the rest: in 2018, OpenAI released GPT ("Generative Pre-trained Transformer") and Google released BERT;
in 2020, GPT-3 had 175 billion parameters.
On November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT. According to UBS, citing data from Similarweb,
it reached about 100 million monthly active users two months later, in January 2023, and was called the fastest-growing consumer app in history.
The analysts wrote: "In 20 years following the internet space, we cannot recall a faster ramp in a consumer internet app."
Look ahead: → Attention and the Transformer, Large language models
Act 12 — 2022–2026: From Talking to Doing
Now there were models that could chat. The next question: could they go and do things on their own?
October 2022: Shunyu Yao and colleagues at Princeton and Google published ReAct: have the model alternate between "thinking" and "acting."
Think one step, call a tool (say, look something up on Wikipedia), see the result, then think about the next step. Almost every agent today runs this loop.
March 30, 2023: Toran Bruce Richards released AutoGPT on GitHub: give GPT-4 a goal and let it break the goal into tasks and carry them out itself.
It collected 30,000 stars in 13 days and passed 100,000 a few weeks later, becoming one of the fastest-growing open-source projects on GitHub at the time.
It often went in circles and failed to get things done, but it showed everyone what agents could be.
April 2023: Joon Sung Park and colleagues at Stanford and Google published "Generative Agents."
They placed 25 residents, each driven by a large language model, in a pixel-art town called Smallville.
The researchers told the café owner, Isabella, just one thing: she wanted to throw a Valentine's Day party.
After that, she sent out the invitations herself, word spread through the town, someone asked their crush to go with them, and that evening several residents actually showed up at the party.
Nobody wrote a "throw a party" script. It "emerged" from the interactions of multiple agents.
November 2024: Anthropic released MCP (Model Context Protocol), a single standard for connecting models to all kinds of tools and data sources.
April 2025: Google released the A2A (Agent2Agent) protocol, which lets agents from different vendors discover each other, talk and work together;
that June, it was donated to the Linux Foundation.
June 2025: Anthropic described how its own multi-agent research system works: a "lead agent" does the planning and sends out several "subagents" to search in parallel,
then pulls the results together. In their internal evaluation, a system with Claude Opus 4 as the lead agent and Claude Sonnet 4 as subagents outperformed Claude Opus 4 working alone by 90.2%; the price was that the multi-agent system used about 15 times as many tokens as an ordinary chat.
February 2026: OpenAI published "Harness engineering": over about five months, a small team
built a product of about 1 million lines of code, without a single line written by a human. AI agents wrote all of it.
The engineers' job became designing the agents' working environment: documentation, rules, tests, checking tools, and loops in which agents review each other's work.
This surrounding system is called the harness.
From the motors turning potentiometers in 1958 to the agents reviewing each other's code in 2026, "learning by doing" has never stopped.
The rest of the chapters in this repo are about building these things yourself, by hand.
Look ahead: → Agents, Harness, Multi-agent collaboration
Epilogue — Three Threads That Run Through It All
Looking back over these seventy years, three threads keep coming up:
1. A battle between two approaches: one camp believed intelligence comes from rules and logic (symbolism: Deep Blue, early AI);
the other believed it comes from connections learned from data (connectionism: the perceptron, backpropagation, deep learning).
In 1969 the first camp had the upper hand; after 2012 the second won across the board. Today's agents combine the two: a model that was learned, calling tools that were written by hand.
2. Data: from 50 cards, to ImageNet's more than a million images, to the better part of the internet that large language models have read.
3. Compute: from the 5-ton IBM 704, to two gaming graphics cards in a bedroom, to today's GPU clusters.
Many of the ideas, like multilayer networks and backpropagation, had been around for a long time; they were just waiting for data and compute to catch up.
The concepts below are listed in the order they appear in the stories, and each one notes the act where it first appears.
1. What Is Machine Learning
In one sentence: instead of writing the rules directly, let the program work out the rules from data (experience) on its own.
Traditional programming: rules written by people + data → answers.
Machine learning: data + answers → the program finds the rules itself.
In 1997, Tom Mitchell gave a more precise definition in his textbook:
A computer program is said to learn from experience E with respect to some class of tasks T and performance measure P,
if its performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E.
Applied to Lab 1: T = telling whether a card is marked on the left or the right, P = accuracy, E = the cards it has seen. The more it sees, the higher its accuracy, so it is "learning."
First appears in: Act 3 (Samuel's checkers)
2. Models, Parameters, Training, Inference
Model: a function with lots of "knobs." Data goes in, predictions come out.
Parameters (weights): those knobs. On the Mark I Perceptron, the parameters really were knobs: 400 potentiometers turned by electric motors.
Today's large models have hundreds of billions of parameters. The principle is the same; there are just far more knobs.
Training: adjusting the knobs bit by bit, based on data, so the predictions get more and more accurate.
Inference: once training is done, the knobs are fixed and the model is used on new data. When you chat with a chatbot, it is doing "inference."
First appears in: Prologue, Act 4
3. Three Ways to Learn
Pre-training large language models also uses a variant: self-supervised learning. The answers are hidden in the data itself:
for example, cover up the next word of a sentence, have the model guess it, then uncover it to check. No human labeling is needed, so you can train on huge amounts of text.
First appears in: Act 3, Act 10
4. Loss Functions
In one sentence: a single number that measures how badly the model is wrong right now.
Lab 3 uses mean squared error: for each example, take "prediction minus correct answer," square it, add them all up, and take the average.
A perfect guess gives a loss of 0, and the more wrong it is, the bigger the loss. The goal of training is to make this number smaller and smaller.
5. Gradient Descent and the Learning Rate
Imagine you're on a mountain in thick fog. You can't see where the valley is; you can only feel which direction is steepest under your feet.
The simplest way down: take a small step in the steepest downhill direction, and repeat many times.
That "steepest direction" is the gradient (the derivative of the loss with respect to each parameter);
How big each step is, is the learning rate. Too small, and the descent is slow; too big, and you might step right over the valley and bounce back and forth between the two sides.
In Lab 3, W -= LR * d_W is that step.
First appears in: Act 6
6. Training Set, Test Set, Generalization and Overfitting
Training set: the data used to tune the parameters. Test set: data the model has never seen, kept just for the exam.
Generalization: the ability to do well on data it hasn't seen. This is what we really want.
Overfitting: doing well on the training set but poorly on new data, like memorizing the answers to the practice problems.
Lab 1 is a ready-made example: after seeing only 50 cards, it gets the whole training set right, but only about 94% of new cards;
after 1,000 more cards, its accuracy on new cards rises to about 99%. More data, and more varied data, usually leads to better generalization. That's what ImageNet was all about.
First appears in: Act 4, Act 8
7. Neurons and the Perceptron
An artificial neuron does two things:
1. Weighted sum: multiply each input by a weight, add them up, then add a bias: z = w1·x1 + w2·x2 + … + b;
2. Activation: if z is greater than 0, output 1 (fire); otherwise output 0 (or -1).
McCulloch–Pitts neuron (1943): the weights are set by people; it doesn't learn.
Perceptron (1958): the weights start at 0 and are learned with one simple rule. When it guesses wrong, w ← w + y·x (y is the correct answer, ±1).
First appears in: Act 1, Act 4
8. Linear Separability and XOR
The perceptron's decision rule, w1·x1 + w2·x2 + b > 0, draws a straight line on the plane: one side says 1, the other side says 0.
So a perceptron can only solve problems that one straight line can separate. This is called being linearly separable.
AND and OR are both linearly separable. XOR's two classes sit on the two diagonals of a square,
so no straight line can separate them, and a single-layer perceptron is doomed never to learn it. Minsky and Papert's book also proved limits more general than this.
First appears in: Act 5 xor_1969.py
9. Multilayer Networks and Activation Functions
The fix is to add one or more hidden layers between the input and the output.
Each layer can re-represent the data "from a new angle," and stacking several layers lets you draw curved, complicated boundaries.
But here's the key: every layer must be followed by a nonlinear activation function (like the sigmoid in Lab 3, or the ReLU that's common today).
Without it, stacked linear computations are still linear, no different from a single layer, and XOR still can't be learned. (That's Think about it question 3 in Lab 3.)
10. Backpropagation
Multilayer networks bring a new problem: when the output is wrong, which weight in which layer is to blame? This is called the "credit assignment problem."
Backpropagation's answer:
1. Forward: compute from the input, layer by layer, to the output;
2. Compute the error: compare with the correct answer to get the loss;
3. Backward: use the chain rule to pass "the derivative of the loss with respect to the output" back, layer by layer, getting the derivative of the loss with respect to every weight (the gradient);
4. Update: each weight takes a small gradient descent step.
Training today's large models with hundreds of billions of parameters still uses these four steps. Frameworks like PyTorch just do step 3 for you automatically.
First appears in: Act 6 backprop_1986.py
11. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)
An ordinary network connects every pixel of an image to every neuron. That's too many parameters, and it knows nothing about the structure of an "image." CNNs have two clever design choices:
Local receptive fields: each neuron looks at only a small patch of the image (say 5×5), hunting for local patterns like edges and corners;
Weight sharing: the same "pattern detector" slides across the whole image. Whether a cat's ear shows up in the top-left or the bottom-right corner, the same detector looks for it.
Shallow layers find lines; deeper layers combine lines into eyes, wheels and faces.
First appears in: Act 7, Act 9
12. Deep Learning and GPUs
Deep learning just means "neural networks with many layers." The "deep" refers to the number of layers.
Most of a neural network's computation is matrix multiplication: thousands upon thousands of independent multiplications and additions.
GPUs were originally designed for game graphics, processing millions of pixels at once, so they're naturally good at this kind of massively parallel computation.
AlexNet proved that when you bring together a big network + big data + GPUs, deep learning can leave traditional methods far behind.
First appears in: Act 9
13. Generative Models and GANs
Most of the models so far are discriminative: given an image, decide whether it's a cat or a dog.
A generative model works the other way around: it learns what the data looks like, then creates new, realistic data.
A GAN pits two networks against each other: the generator makes fakes, and the discriminator tells real from fake. Each pushes the other to improve, like a counterfeiter and the person checking for fake bills.
Today's image generation mostly uses "diffusion models," and large language models are essentially generative models too: they generate the next word.
First appears in: Act 10
14. Attention and the Transformer
When you read the word "it," you automatically look back to find what "it" refers to. That's exactly what the attention mechanism does:
as it processes each word, the model gives every other word in the sentence a "relevance" score, then pulls in their information, weighted by those scores.
The Transformer is a network built by stacking attention. It has two advantages:
any two words in a sentence can "talk" directly, no matter how far apart they are;
all the words can be computed at the same time, which suits GPUs perfectly, so models can be made enormous.
Almost every large language model today (GPT, Claude, DeepSeek…) is a Transformer.

15. Large Language Models (LLMs)
Token: the smallest unit a model reads and writes. It might be a single character, a word, or part of a word.
Pre-training: reading huge amounts of text and practicing "guess the next token" over and over (self-supervised learning). This step is where the model picks up language, knowledge, and some ability to reason.
Fine-tuning: continuing training on smaller, more targeted data, for example to teach it to answer in a "conversation" format.
RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback): people rank several of the model's answers, and reinforcement learning then makes the model lean toward the answers people prefer.
Context window: the maximum number of tokens the model can "see" at once. Anything beyond that, it can't see.
A common misunderstanding: a large model's API itself has no memory. On every call, you have to send it the full conversation history again.
This is the key to understanding agents and multi-agent systems, and the side quest covers it in detail.

17. Harness
A "harness" originally means the gear for a horse: the reins, straps and all the rest, which let the horse's strength be guided in the right direction.
In AI, the model is the horse, and the harness is everything wrapped around the model that makes it work reliably:
the while loop itself;

18. Multi-Agent Collaboration
When one agent isn't enough, have several agents split up the work:
Orchestrator–worker: a lead agent breaks down the task, hands out the work and pulls the results together, while several subagents work in parallel;
Writer–reviewer: one writes, the other finds fault;
Routing: hand each type of task to the model that's best at it;
Debate: several models each give their view and argue against each other, then reach a conclusion.
Why split up the work? Because each agent's context window is limited, so a task that's too big won't fit in one agent; and independent subtasks can be done in parallel.
The price is that token use multiplies, for example the roughly 15x that Anthropic reported. So it's only worth it when the task is valuable enough.

Chapter 2 — Linear Models and Gradient Descent: Starting with a Lost Planet
Chapter 1 was about where neural networks came from. But the "engine" neural networks use to learn,
namely define an error, then drive that error as low as it will go, is far older than neural networks.
Its story begins on New Year's Day 1801, with a lost planet.
How to read this chapter: As in Chapter 1, read the stories first, then run the labs, and save the Concepts for last.
Prologue — New Year's Day 1801, Palermo: A "Star" That Moved
On January 1, 1801, the first night of the 19th century, the Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi,
at the observatory in Palermo, Sicily, noticed a faint point of light. When he looked again the next night, it had moved a little.
It wasn't a star. It was a body orbiting the Sun between Mars and Jupiter, later named Ceres.
Piazzi tracked it for 41 days and recorded about two dozen observations. Then it drew close to the Sun and vanished into the Sun's glare.
When it came back out from behind the Sun, where in that vast sky should anyone look? A few dozen observations covered only a short arc of its orbit,
and at the time there was no reliable way to compute a full orbit from so little data. Europe's astronomers searched for months and found nothing.
That's when a 24-year-old German, Carl Friedrich Gauss, took on the problem.
He spent about three months on the calculations and worked out where Ceres should appear in December.
On December 31, 1801, the astronomer Franz Xaver von Zach, searching from Gotha where Gauss had predicted,
found Ceres again less than half a degree from the predicted position. The next day, Heinrich Olbers saw it too, in Bremen.
Discovered on New Year's Day, found again on New Year's Eve. Overnight, Gauss was famous all over Europe.
One of his secret weapons was a method for handling "observations with errors": what came to be called the method of least squares.
Try it: run ceres_1801.py. Use 24 noisy observations to predict the position a year later,
and compare which is more accurate: "just two points" or "least squares."
Act 1 — 1805–1820: Who Really Invented Least Squares?
In 1805, the French mathematician Adrien-Marie Legendre published a book on computing the orbits of comets.
Its appendix (dated March 6, 1805) clearly laid out a method:
when there are more equations than unknowns, and every observation has some error, choose the solution that makes the sum of the squares of all the errors as small as possible.
He gave the method a name: the method of least squares (méthode des moindres quarrés).
Four years later, in 1809, Gauss published his great work on celestial mechanics, Theoria Motus. It also covered least squares
and mentioned Legendre, but it also said that he had been using this method since 1795.
Legendre was furious. On May 31, 1809, he wrote to Gauss, saying that priority should be settled by publication.
In 1820, he publicly rebutted Gauss again in a supplement to one of his works. Gauss later tried to prove that he really had been first,
but by his own account, his old calculation notes had been lost, and his colleagues either couldn't remember or didn't want to be drawn into the dispute.
Today, textbooks usually credit both: Legendre was the first to publish and name it, while Gauss connected it to a probability theory of errors (what became the "normal distribution")
and explained why you should use squares.
Look ahead: Why "squares," of all things? → Residuals, least squares and mean squared error
Act 2 — 1847: Cauchy's "Downhill Method"
For simple models like a straight line, least squares has a ready-made formula. But real orbit problems are nonlinear:
a celestial body's orbit has 6 unknowns, the equations are full of trigonometric functions, and there's simply no formula that solves them.
In 1847, the French mathematician Augustin-Louis Cauchy published a short note, just three pages long, in the Comptes Rendus of the French Academy of Sciences,
titled Méthode générale pour la résolution des systèmes d'équations simultanées ("General method for solving systems of simultaneous equations"). His starting point was exactly the orbit calculations of astronomy.
His idea was astonishingly simple:
1. Combine "the errors of all the equations" into one total error, which is a function of the unknowns;
2. From where you are now, see which direction makes the total error drop fastest;
3. Take a small step in that direction, then repeat.
This is gradient descent. More than 170 years later, models like ChatGPT and Claude are still trained with its descendants.
Try it: run gradient_descent_1847.py. Walk downhill on Galton's real data,
and see what happens when the learning rate is too small, just right, or too big, and why "centering the data first" can make it hundreds of times faster.
Look ahead: → The gradient, Gradient descent and the learning rate, Feature scaling
Act 3 — 1875–1886: Sweet Peas, Heights, and the Odd Name "Regression"
Francis Galton was Darwin's cousin, a Victorian scholar who wanted to measure just about everything.
He wanted to know: how are parents' traits passed on to their children?
In 1875, he ran a very creative experiment. He sorted sweet pea seeds by weight into 7 grades, from very large to very small,
put 10 seeds of each grade into packets, and mailed them to 7 friends, asking them to plant the seeds and send back the next generation of seeds they harvested.
What he found: the offspring of large seeds were also on the large side, but not as large as their parents; the offspring of small seeds were also on the small side, but not as small as their parents.
The offspring had all "gone back" some of the way toward the average.
In 1886, he confirmed the same phenomenon with human height. He collected the heights of 928 adult children from 205 families
and published the paper Regression towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature.
When parents were somewhat taller than average, their children were, on average, taller by only about two-thirds of that gap.
He called this phenomenon regression (literally, "going back"). Later, the whole set of methods for "describing the relationship between two quantities with a straight line"
took over the name, and we still use it today, even though most problems have nothing to do with "going back."
(Galton's history also has a dark side: he coined the word "eugenics," an idea that later caused serious harm.)
Try it: run galton_1886.py. It uses Galton's real data from back then.
You'll compute that "about two-thirds" yourself, and you'll discover that regression to the mean is a purely statistical phenomenon.

Act 4 — 1951: Reaching the Finish Line, Noise and All
Every step of gradient descent requires computing the "exact" downhill direction. But what if every measurement is noisy, and all you can get is a rough direction?
In 1951, the statisticians Herbert Robbins and Sutton Monro
published A Stochastic Approximation Method. They proved that even if the direction of every step is noisy, as long as the step size shrinks gradually in the right way,
you'll still reach the right answer in the end.
This very "theoretical"-looking paper later became the theoretical foundation of stochastic gradient descent (SGD).
Look ahead: → Batch, stochastic and mini-batch, Learning rate decay
Act 5 — 1959–1960: Stanford's ADALINE
At the same time Rosenblatt was building the perceptron at Cornell (see Chapter 1), a young Stanford professor, Bernard Widrow,
and his first PhD student, Marcian "Ted" Hoff, came up with a different learning rule in the fall of 1959.
The perceptron adjusts only when it guesses wrong, and each adjustment is a fixed size. Widrow and Hoff's rule:
for every example that comes in, adjust the weights in proportion to "how wrong" it was. Very wrong, big adjustment; slightly wrong, small adjustment.
This rule is called LMS (least mean squares), also known as the Widrow–Hoff rule or the delta rule. At heart, it's just gradient descent that looks at one example at a time.
They used it to build a device called ADALINE (adaptive linear neuron).
LMS later became a huge success in signal processing, widely used in adaptive filtering such as echo cancellation on telephone lines.
And that PhD student, Ted Hoff, later became Intel's employee number 12
and led the design of the Intel 4004, released in November 1971: the world's first commercial microprocessor.
Try it: run lms_1960.py. Compare "looking at all the data every step" with "looking at one example at a time":
after seeing the same amount of data, which one is closer to the answer?
Look ahead: → LMS and the perceptron
Act 6 — 1958–2014: From Regression to Classification, from SGD to Adam
Linear regression predicts a number (a height, a position). But many problems need a yes or no: Is this email spam? Is the mark on this card on the left or the right?
In 1958, the British statistician David Cox systematically laid out logistic regression:
first compute the linear model's output, then use an S-shaped curve (the logistic function, which is the sigmoid from Chapter 1's Lab 3) to squash it into the range 0 to 1, and treat that as the probability of "yes."
It is still one of the most widely used classification methods, and it's also the standard form of a single neuron in a neural network.
Gradient descent itself kept evolving too. In December 2014, Diederik Kingma and Jimmy Ba published the Adam optimizer:
it automatically adjusts the step size for each parameter and also takes the directions of the previous few steps into account, so noise is less likely to throw it off course.
Adam quickly became one of the most widely used optimizers in deep learning, and today most large language models are trained with variants from the Adam family.
From Cauchy's three-page note in 1847 to training hundreds of billions of parameters today, the core step has never changed: compute the gradient, take a small step downhill.

Epilogue — Two Ways to Find the Answer
This chapter showed two ways of "finding the best parameters":
1. Solve the formula directly (Legendre, Gauss): for linear models, least squares has an exact closed-form solution that gets there in one step.
2. Walk there step by step (Cauchy, Robbins–Monro, Widrow–Hoff): no formula needed, just the ability to compute gradients.
Why does deep learning today use only the second? Because neural networks are nonlinear and simply have no closed-form solution;
and when there are hundreds of billions of parameters and trillions of tokens of data, you couldn't compute it even if there were a formula.
Only gradient descent, especially stochastic gradient descent, can handle that kind of scale.
About the data: data/galton_1886.csv is the original data from Galton's 1886 paper (928 rows),
transcribed from the R package HistData, maintained by the statistician Michael Friendly.
1. Regression and Linear Models
In one sentence: regression means predicting a number from inputs; a linear model means "multiply each input by a weight, then add them up."
x (features): the input information, like the parents' heights;
w (weights): how much each feature matters, like 0.65;
b (bias/intercept): the baseline value when all the features are 0.
Chapter 1's perceptron is a linear model too; it just adds one final step: "greater than 0, output 1; otherwise output 0."
First appears in: Act 3
2. What Exactly Is "Linear" About a Linear Model?
The "linear" in linear model means linear in the parameters (w, b), not in the inputs.
So y = w1·x + w2·x² + b is a linear model too: just treat x² as a new feature.
This lets linear models fit curves while keeping least squares' closed-form solution.
The power of neural networks is that they can learn on their own which features to use (the hidden layer in Chapter 1's Lab 3), so no one has to design x² by hand.
3. Residuals, Least Squares and Mean Squared Error
Residual: actual value − predicted value, that is, how far off each point is.
Least squares: choose the parameters that make the sum of squared residuals as small as possible.
Mean squared error (MSE): the average of the squared residuals, the most common loss function for regression.
Why squares?
1. Positive and negative errors don't cancel each other out;
2. Big errors get an extra-heavy penalty, so the model works hard to avoid wild mistakes;
3. The square function is differentiable everywhere, so you can take derivatives and solve for a formula directly (the normal equations), and it also makes gradient descent easy;
4. Gauss proved that if the observation errors follow a normal distribution, least squares gives the "most likely" answer.
The downside is that it's very sensitive to outliers: one wildly off data point can drag the whole line toward it. In that case you can switch to other loss functions, such as absolute error.
First appears in: Prologue, Act 1 ceres_1801.py
4. The Normal Equations (Closed-Form Solution)
For a linear model, take the derivative of the mean squared error with respect to each parameter and set it to 0, and you get a set of equations called the normal equations:
Solving them gives the least squares answer. The 2×2 system solved by hand in Lab 1 is their simplest form.
The downsides: with d features, the work needed to solve them grows roughly in proportion to d³, so with many features it becomes too much to compute; and nonlinear models have no such formula at all.
5. The Gradient
The gradient is a vector that points in "the direction in which the function rises fastest," and its length tells you how steep it is.
Each of its components is the partial derivative of the loss with respect to one parameter: how much the loss changes if that parameter goes up a tiny bit.
Walk in the opposite direction of the gradient, and the loss drops fastest.
6. Gradient Descent and the Learning Rate
Learning rate too small: every step is steady, but it takes forever;
Learning rate just right: it reaches the bottom of the valley quickly;
Learning rate too big: one step overshoots the bottom of the valley and lands higher up on the other side, it jumps farther and farther, and finally it "blows up."
The upper limit on the learning rate is set by how steep the loss function is in its steepest direction: the steeper it is, the smaller the steps have to be.
In Lab 2, going from "a bit big" to "too big" changes the learning rate only a tiny bit, yet one converges and the other diverges. That's because it crosses this limit.
First appears in: Act 2 gradient_descent_1847.py
7. Feature Scaling
If one direction is very steep and another is very flat, the loss function looks like a long, narrow canyon.
The learning rate has to be set for the steep direction (otherwise it diverges), and as a result it can barely move along the flat direction.
Centering (subtracting the mean) and standardizing (then also dividing by the standard deviation) turn the canyon into a nice round bowl, and gradient descent gets much faster.
In Lab 2, the raw data's canyon is millions of times steeper in one direction than in the other; after centering, the difference is only 3 times.
That's also why the first step of almost every machine learning pipeline is to scale the data to similar ranges.
8. Batch, Stochastic and Mini-Batch Gradient Descent
Epoch: one complete pass through all the training data is called an epoch.
First appears in: Act 4, Act 5 lms_1960.py
9. Learning Rate Decay
SGD's direction is noisy, so near the finish line it keeps jittering around.
If you make the learning rate shrink gradually, the jitter shrinks too, and in the end it settles firmly on the answer.
What Robbins and Monro proved in 1951 was exactly how the step size should shrink to guarantee convergence.
When large models are trained today, the learning rate is usually "warmed up" first (slowly raised from a small value), then gradually decayed.
10. Regression to the Mean
When two quantities aren't perfectly correlated and one of them is extreme, the other tends to be less extreme, closer to the average.
This isn't some mysterious force; it's a statistical regularity: an extreme value usually includes a share of "luck," and luck isn't inherited and doesn't last.
Many "effects" in everyday life are really illusions caused by regression to the mean: the improvement after an especially bad exam, the slump after an especially good performance (the "cover jinx").
To judge whether a method really works, you need a control group; a before-and-after comparison isn't enough.
First appears in: Act 3 galton_1886.py
11. The Danger of Extrapolation
Predicting inside the range your data covers is called interpolation, and it's fairly reliable; predicting outside that range is called extrapolation, and it's dangerous.
Lab 1 uses 41 days of data to predict day 364. It works because the form of model we used (motion at a constant speed) happens to be right.
Gauss could find Ceres again because he, too, used the right physical model (Kepler's elliptical orbits).
If the form of the model itself is wrong, then no matter how well it fits the data, extrapolation can go wildly wrong.
12. From Regression to Classification: Logistic Regression
Logistic regression = linear model + sigmoid function:
The sigmoid squashes any real number into the range 0–1, which is treated as the probability of "yes." Training usually doesn't use mean squared error,
but cross-entropy loss instead: if the true answer is "yes," the lower the probability the model gives to "yes," the heavier the penalty.
A logistic regression is really just "a neuron that outputs a probability." Connect lots of these neurons in layers, and you get the neural networks of Chapter 1.

13. Optimizers: Momentum and Adam
Plain gradient descent looks only at the gradient of "the current step." There are two main kinds of improvements:
Momentum: like a ball rolling downhill, it keeps some of its earlier speed. In a canyon it's less likely to bang back and forth, and on flat ground it can keep rolling forward.
Adaptive learning rates: give each parameter its own step size. Parameters that often swing wildly take smaller steps; parameters that rarely get updated take bigger ones.
Adam combines the two and works with hardly any tuning, so it became the default choice in deep learning.

14. LMS and the Perceptron
LMS shows us that a learning rule can be derived from "minimizing a loss function," instead of being designed by intuition.
Backpropagation in 1986 extended this idea to multilayer networks.

Chapter 3 — Neural Networks and Autodiff: Teaching Machines to Take Their Own Derivatives
In Chapter 1 we wrote backpropagation for the XOR network by hand, and we had to work out every derivative ourselves.
That's fine for a three-layer network. But what about a hundred layers and hundreds of billions of parameters?
This chapter is about how people handed the job of "taking derivatives" over to the machine completely, and why deep networks are so hard to train.
How to read this chapter: Read the stories first, then run the labs, and save the Concepts for last. These labs need only Python and NumPy, so no API key is needed.
Prologue — 1970, Copenhagen: An Afternoon in the Park
In 1970, Seppo Linnainmaa, a student at the University of Helsinki in Finland, was writing his master's thesis.
His problem had nothing to do with neural networks. Every time a computer does a floating-point operation, it makes a tiny rounding error.
As those little errors pile up step after step, how far off does the final result end up?
To answer that, you need to know how sensitive the final result is to every intermediate step. In other words, a whole pile of derivatives.
According to a later account by the mathematician Andreas Griewank, Linnainmaa said
the solution came to him on a sunny afternoon in 1970, in a park in Copenhagen:
record the computation, then start from the final result and walk through it backward, using the chain rule to pass the sensitivities back one step at a time.
A single backward pass gives you the derivative of the result with respect to every intermediate quantity. He even implemented it in FORTRAN.
This is reverse-mode automatic differentiation. More than a decade later, people used it to train neural networks and gave it another name: backpropagation.
Try it: run autograd.py. It implements an autodiff engine in under 100 lines of code,
draws the computational graph, and uses "numerical gradients" to check that it computes correctly.
Look ahead: → Computational graphs, Forward mode and reverse mode
Act 1 — 1989: Is One Layer Enough?
As Chapter 1 told it, in 1986 backpropagation made multi-layer networks hot again. But one basic question was still unanswered:
how complicated a thing can a multi-layer network actually represent?
In 1989, two papers gave the answer almost at the same time. The mathematician George Cybenko
proved in Mathematics of Control, Signals and Systems that with just one hidden layer and an S-shaped activation function like the sigmoid,
a network can approximate any continuous function to any accuracy you like. The same year, Kurt Hornik, Stinchcombe and White
published a paper with a resounding title in Neural Networks: Multilayer Feedforward Networks are Universal Approximators.
This is the universal approximation theorem. It is reassuring: in principle, there is no shape a neural network "can't draw."
But two things it doesn't say went on to become problems for decades: how many neurons do you need? And how do you actually train the weights?
Try it: run mlp_moons.py. Use the engine you wrote yourself to train a network
that separates two interlocking "moons." A network with no hidden layer can only draw a straight line; add hidden layers, and the boundary starts to bend.

Act 2 — 1991, Munich: The Deeper, the Harder to Learn
On June 15, 1991, Sepp Hochreiter, a student at the Technical University of Munich, turned in his diploma thesis (Diplom).
It was titled Untersuchungen zu dynamischen neuronalen Netzen ("Studies of Dynamic Neural Networks"), it was written in German, and his advisor was Jürgen Schmidhuber.
The thesis analyzed a phenomenon that gave everyone headaches. As the error signal travels backward through the network, it gets multiplied by some factors at every layer.
If those factors are mostly smaller than 1, after a few dozen layers the signal is almost zero, and the layers near the input learn nothing at all. This is called vanishing gradients.
If they are mostly larger than 1, the signal grows bigger and bigger as it travels. This is called exploding gradients.
Schmidhuber later called this thesis one of the most important documents in the history of machine learning.
Six years later, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber invented the LSTM precisely to get around this problem (more on that in Chapter 4).
Try it: run vanishing_gradient.py. Build a 30-layer network
and see how much of the error signal is left when it gets back to layer 1: the sigmoid network keeps only 10⁻²⁰ of it, while a network with too large an initialization amplifies it more than ten thousand times.

Act 3 — 2010–2015: A New Activation Function and the Right Starting Weights
All through the 2000s, deep networks were known for being "hard to train." Around 2010, a few improvements that looked very plain turned the situation around completely:
In 2010, Xavier Glorot and Yoshua Bengio of the University of Montreal
published Understanding the Difficulty of Training Deep Feedforward Neural Networks. They carefully analyzed how the signal grows and shrinks from layer to layer,
and proposed scaling the initial weights by "the number of inputs and outputs." This later became known as Xavier initialization.
In 2010, Vinod Nair and Hinton at the University of Toronto proposed at ICML improving models with rectified linear units (ReLU).
The rule could hardly be simpler: if the input is above 0, pass it through unchanged; if it is below 0, output 0.
In 2011, Deep Sparse Rectifier Neural Networks by Glorot, Bordes and Bengio showed that deep networks using ReLU
can train very well even without complicated pre-training tricks.
In 2012, the AlexNet paper reported that on the CIFAR-10 dataset, a network with ReLU reached a 25% training error rate
six times faster than one with tanh. From then on, ReLU became the default choice.
In 2015, Kaiming He and colleagues at Microsoft Research Asia published Delving Deep into Rectifiers. It proposed an initialization method designed specifically for ReLU
(He initialization), which made it possible to train very deep networks from scratch. The paper's model reached a 4.94% top-5 error rate on ImageNet,
the first result to beat human-level performance (5.1%) on this challenge.
In Lab 2 you'll see for yourself how much initialization matters: same network, same data, but if you pick too small a scale for the initial weights,
accuracy gets stuck at around 90%; switch to He initialization, and it reaches 100% in 80 steps.

Act 4 — 2012: A Lesson from the Bank Tellers
The bigger the network, the easier it is to overfit: it memorizes the training data and then falls apart on new data.
As Hinton himself recalled in an online Q&A (on Reddit), he once went to the bank and noticed that the tellers kept changing. He asked one of them why.
The teller didn't know, only that they got moved around a lot. Hinton figured it was probably because defrauding the bank would take several employees working together;
if they were rotated often, they couldn't conspire. That gave him an idea: at every training step, randomly "switch off" some of the neurons.
Then they can't form fragile little "cliques," and the network is less likely to overfit.
In July 2012, Hinton and his students posted Improving Neural Networks by Preventing Co-adaptation of Feature Detectors on arXiv.
The method is called Dropout. AlexNet used it the same year.

Act 5 — 2007–2017: No More Derivatives by Hand
In Chapter 1's Lab 3, we had to work out every backpropagation formula ourselves. Change the network structure even a little, and you have to derive it all over again. It is very easy to make mistakes.
Deep learning frameworks changed all that. At their core is exactly Linnainmaa's reverse-mode automatic differentiation:
you just write the forward computation, and the framework records the computational graph automatically and then computes all the gradients for you.
In 2007, a research team at the University of Montreal (later MILA) released Theano, one of the first Python frameworks to support automatic differentiation and GPUs;
On November 9, 2015, Google open-sourced TensorFlow;
In January 2017, Facebook's research team publicly released PyTorch. Its "build the graph as the code runs" approach is just as intuitive as our Value class,
and it quickly became researchers' favorite tool.
From then on, researchers could spend their energy designing new network architectures instead of deriving formulas.

As frameworks grew more powerful, their insides grew more complicated too, and it became hard for beginners to see what they were actually doing.
In 2020, Andrej Karpathy, former head of AI at Tesla and one of the founding members of OpenAI,
released micrograd on GitHub: an autodiff engine of about 100 lines, plus a neural network library of about 50 lines,

Epilogue — Three Questions
The three stories in this chapter match three key questions in training neural networks:
1. How do we take derivatives? Reverse-mode automatic differentiation: one backward pass gives you all the gradients;
2. Can a network represent the answer? The universal approximation theorem says yes, but it takes enough neurons and the right training;
3. Can it be trained? Vanishing and exploding gradients once stopped deep networks in their tracks; ReLU and good initialization (and later residual connections) cleared the way.
In the next chapter, we'll see how researchers designed network architectures specifically for images and language: from convolutional networks all the way to the Transformer.
1. Computational graphs
Any computation can be broken down into small steps: add, multiply, tanh... Draw them as a graph, with each node a value
and each edge saying "who was computed from whom," and you have a computational graph. In Lab 1, a Value object's _prev holds its incoming edges.
The first thing a framework does is quietly record this graph while you write the forward computation.
First appears in: Prologue autograd.py
2. The chain rule
If L depends on d, and d depends on e, then dL/de = dL/dd × dd/de.
Backpropagation is just the chain rule applied over and over on the computational graph: each node only needs to know "its own local derivative,"
multiply that by the gradient arriving from the nodes after it, and pass the result on to the nodes before it. The local derivative of each operation is simple:
Addition: pass it unchanged to both sides;
Multiplication: multiply by the other side's value;
tanh: multiply by 1 - tanh².
3. Forward mode and reverse mode
Automatic differentiation can run in two directions:
Forward mode: start from one input and compute the derivatives of all the results with respect to that one input. With n inputs, you need n passes.
Reverse mode: start from one result, walk backward once, and get the derivatives of that one result with respect to all the inputs.
Here is the situation in a neural network: millions to hundreds of billions of inputs (parameters), and only 1 result (the loss). So reverse mode wins hands down. That is backpropagation.
First appears in: Prologue
4. Gradient checking
A numerical gradient takes the derivative the dumbest way possible: nudge one parameter a tiny bit (say, 10⁻⁶) and see how much the loss changes.
It is slow (every parameter needs two separate evaluations), but it almost never goes wrong, so it is used specifically to check whether your autodiff is correct.
In Lab 1, the two methods differ on the order of 10⁻¹⁰, which shows the engine is right.
5. The universal approximation theorem
With enough neurons in the hidden layer, a network with one hidden layer can approximate any continuous function to any accuracy.
It describes a neural network's expressive power, but it doesn't say how many neurons you need, or whether a training algorithm can find those weights.
In practice, "deeper" is often far more efficient than "wider": a multi-layer network can combine features layer by layer into more and more complex ones.
First appears in: Act 1 mlp_moons.py
6. Activation functions
Without an activation function (or with only linear ones), a multi-layer network is no better than a single layer. The activation function supplies the nonlinearity that lets the network draw curved boundaries.
Today's large models mostly use smooth versions of ReLU, such as GELU and SwiGLU.
First appears in: Act 3
7. Vanishing and exploding gradients
During backpropagation, every time the gradient passes through a layer, it is multiplied by "this layer's weights" and "the derivative of the activation function."
If these factors are mostly smaller than 1 → multiplied dozens of times, they head toward 0 → vanishing gradients, and the early layers can't learn;
If they are mostly larger than 1 → multiplied dozens of times, they head toward infinity → exploding gradients, and training simply diverges.
The fixes include ReLU-style activation functions, good initialization, normalization layers, residual connections (Chapter 4), and the LSTM, designed specifically for sequences (Chapter 4).
For exploding gradients there is also a quick and blunt fix: gradient clipping, which just shrinks the gradient back under a cap when it gets too big.
First appears in: Act 2 vanishing_gradient.py
8. Initialization
Before training starts, the weights can't all be 0: then every neuron would be identical and could never learn to be different. So we initialize them randomly.
But the scale of the random numbers is crucial:
Too small → the signal gets weaker layer by layer → vanishing gradients;
Too large → the signal gets stronger layer by layer → exploding gradients.
The usual rule is to scale by "the number of inputs, n":
Xavier initialization (standard deviation about √(1/n)): suits tanh and sigmoid;
He initialization (standard deviation √(2/n)): suits ReLU. ReLU zeroes out half of the signal, so the scale is a bit bigger to make up for it.
First appears in: Act 3 mlp_moons.py, vanishing_gradient.py
9. Overfitting, regularization and Dropout
Overfitting means learning the training data too well and then doing worse on new data (Chapter 1, concept 6). The methods for fighting it are collectively called regularization:
L2 regularization (weight decay): add "the sum of the squared weights" to the loss, which nudges the weights to stay small and the model to stay simple;
Dropout: at every training step, randomly switch off some of the neurons (say, half), forcing the network not to rely on any particular "clique";
at test time, switch them all back on. You can think of it as training many sub-networks at once and then averaging them;
Early stopping: stop training once performance on the validation set starts getting worse;
More data: the most fundamental fix of all.
First appears in: Act 4
10. Deep learning frameworks
Frameworks like PyTorch, TensorFlow and JAX do the same core job as Lab 1's Value class: record the computational graph and backpropagate automatically.
The differences:
They work on tensors (multi-dimensional arrays), not individual scalars. One matrix multiplication can replace thousands upon thousands of scalar operations;
The computation can run in parallel on a GPU;
They come with ready-made layers, optimizers (like Adam from Chapter 2) and data-loading tools.
In Lab 2, training 151 parameters with pure-Python scalars takes half a minute. PyTorch on a GPU does the same thing in the blink of an eye. That is the power of vectorization and hardware acceleration.
First appears in: Act 5, Act 6
Chapter 4 — From CNNs to Transformers: Giving Networks Eyes and Attention
The networks of Chapter 3 are "universal," but faced with pictures and sentences, they don't know how to look.
This chapter is about how researchers took inspiration from a cat's brain to design convolutional networks made for seeing images;
and how, step by step, they solved the problem of "forgetting things while reading a long sentence," arriving at the architecture every large model shares today: the Transformer.
How to read this chapter: Read the stories first, then run the labs, and save the Concepts for last. These labs need only Python and NumPy, so no API key is needed.
Prologue — 1959: The Edge of a Slide
In the late 1950s, in a lab at Johns Hopkins University, two young neurophysiologists,
David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel, wanted to find out what the individual neurons in a cat's visual cortex were actually "looking at."
They put electrodes into the visual cortex of an anesthetized cat and used a projector to throw all kinds of patterns onto a screen: bright spots, dark spots... The neurons didn't respond at all.
As Hubel recalled in his Nobel lecture, they tried for hours with no result. Then, just as they were sliding a glass slide into the projector,
the loudspeaker suddenly burst into the dense crackle of a neuron firing like a machine gun.
What excited it wasn't the pattern on the slide but the line cast on the screen by the edge of the slide.
From this they discovered that some neurons in the visual cortex ("simple cells") respond only to edges at a particular orientation.
Turn the line to a different angle, and the response gets weaker. Other neurons ("complex cells") respond to edges at the same orientation but don't care exactly where they appear.
Vision is processed in layers: first find the edges, then combine edges into more complex shapes.
In 1981, the two of them won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for these discoveries.
Try it: run convolution.py. Write a 3×3 "edge detector" by hand,
then recreate Hubel and Wiesel's experiment: show it lines at different angles and plot its "orientation tuning curve."
Look ahead: → Convolution
Act 1 — 1980, Tokyo: The Neocognitron
Kunihiko Fukushima, a researcher at NHK's broadcasting technology research laboratories in Japan, carried Hubel and Wiesel's findings straight into neural networks.
In 1980 he published the Neocognitron in the journal Biological Cybernetics, and its goal was written right into the paper's title:
"a Mechanism of Pattern Recognition Unaffected by Shift in Position."
His network alternated between two kinds of layers:
S layers imitate simple cells, using small local detectors to look for particular patterns;
C layers imitate complex cells, merging nearby responses so that recognition doesn't care about a pattern's exact position.
This is the early form of the "convolution layer + pooling layer" in today's convolutional networks. But the Neocognitron wasn't trained with backpropagation.
As Chapter 1 told it, it was in 1989 that Yann LeCun at Bell Labs combined a similar structure with backpropagation, and that gave us the convolutional neural network (CNN) in the full sense.
Look ahead: → Pooling and translation invariance
Act 2 — 1989–2015: Deeper and Deeper Convolutional Networks
Earlier chapters have already told part of what happened to convolutional networks next: LeCun's networks reading checks, and AlexNet stunning everyone on ImageNet in 2012.
After that, networks kept getting deeper, and also harder and harder to train (the vanishing gradients of Chapter 3).
In 2015, Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren and Jian Sun at Microsoft Research Asia introduced the residual network (ResNet).
The idea is surprisingly simple: instead of outputting a new result directly, each layer outputs "a change to its input," and then the input is added back unchanged:
output = input + f(input). This "shortcut" lets signals and gradients pass through many layers without obstruction.
They trained a network with 152 layers and won the classification task of the 2015 ImageNet challenge (ILSVRC) with a 3.57% error rate.
Residual connections later became standard equipment in almost every deep network, the Transformer included.
Try it: run transformer_block.py and look at part 3. Stack 48 layers and compare having residual connections with not having them.
Look ahead: → Residual connections
Act 3 — 1997: The Forgetful Recurrent Network, and a Conveyor Belt
Images are two-dimensional, but language is a sequence, one thing after another. The most natural way to handle a sequence is a recurrent neural network (RNN):
like a person reading a book, it reads one word after another, keeps "what it has understood so far" in a hidden state, and updates it with every new word.
The problem is exactly what that 1991 diploma thesis from Chapter 3 found: for information from word 1 to reach word 100, it has to go through 99 updates,
each one discounting it a little, until almost nothing is left. RNNs have a hard time remembering long-range relationships.
In 1997, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber published long short-term memory (LSTM) in Neural Computation.
It added a "conveyor belt" to the RNN (the paper calls it the "constant error carousel"), and used a few "gates" to control what gets written onto the belt, what gets erased, and what gets read.
As long as the "forget gate" stays open, information can travel a long way along the belt almost without loss. The paper showed that an LSTM can learn dependencies spanning more than 1,000 time steps.
For nearly twenty years after that, the LSTM was the workhorse for sequence data such as speech and text.
Try it: run rnn_vs_attention.py. Compare how much word 1 influences word 100:
a plain RNN keeps only 10⁻⁴⁸, an LSTM still keeps 0.37, and with attention it is always just one step away.
Look ahead: → RNNs and LSTMs
Act 4 — 2014: The Bottleneck, and "Looking Back"
In 2014, Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc V. Le at Google published the seq2seq (sequence-to-sequence) model:
one LSTM "reads" a whole English sentence into a fixed-length vector, and another LSTM "writes" that vector out as French.
It reached a BLEU score of 34.8 on English-to-French translation, showing the promise of neural networks for translation.
But the design has an obvious bottleneck: however long the sentence, it has to be squeezed into a vector of the same length. Once sentences get long, translation quality drops noticeably.
The same year, Dzmitry Bahdanau, a Belarusian student interning in Bengio's lab at the University of Montreal,
published Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate together with Kyunghyun Cho and Bengio.
Their approach: instead of squeezing the whole sentence into one vector, keep a representation of every word in the source sentence; every time the model produces a word of the translation, it "looks back" over the source sentence,
gives each source word a weight according to how relevant it is, and takes the weighted average.
This is the attention mechanism. According to Bahdanau's own recollection in a 2024 email to Karpathy, the name "attention" came from Bengio.
Two years later, these techniques entered the lives of hundreds of millions of people. In September 2016, Google released GNMT, a neural machine translation system built on LSTMs and attention.
The first language pair to go live was the notoriously hard Chinese to English: all Chinese-to-English translation on the Google Translate website and mobile app switched to GNMT, about 18 million translations a day.
Try it: run attention.py. Think of attention as a "fuzzy dictionary lookup," then let the word "it" in a sentence find "kitten" on its own.
Look ahead: → seq2seq and the bottleneck, Attention
Act 5 — 2017: Attention Is All You Need
Chapter 1 told the story of the Transformer paper's eight authors and where its name came from. Here we look at what it did technically.
Bahdanau's attention was still built on top of an RNN: the RNN read one word at a time, and attention was only a helper.
The 2017 Transformer took the most radical step: throw out recurrence completely, and build the whole network from attention alone.
Self-attention: every word in the sentence looks at all the other words at the same time, so a connection between any two words takes a single step;
Multiple heads: several sets of attention run at once, each watching a different relationship;
Positional encoding: attention itself doesn't know the order of the words, so each position gets its own unique "number tag" added on;
Residual connections + layer normalization: these let the network be stacked very deep (from earlier work such as ResNet).
Since nothing has to be computed step by step in order anymore, a whole sentence can be handed to the GPU at once and processed in parallel. The paper's base model trained for only 12 hours on 8 P100 GPUs,
and the big model trained for 3.5 days, reaching a BLEU score of 28.4 on English-to-German translation, better than every model at the time, including ensembles of several models.
Try it: run transformer_block.py. Assemble the parts into a complete Transformer,
and see the shape of a sentence as it flows through each layer, and where all the parameters go.
Look ahead: → Self-attention and multi-head attention, Positional encoding, The Transformer block
Act 6 — 2020: An Image Is Worth 16×16 Words
The Transformer was originally designed for language. In October 2020, Alexey Dosovitskiy and colleagues at Google
published a paper with a fun title: An Image is Worth 16x16 Words.
They cut an image into small 16×16-pixel squares, treated each square as a "word," and handed them straight to a Transformer.
No convolutions, no structure designed especially for images. The result: given enough pre-training data, this Vision Transformer (ViT)
matched or even beat the best convolutional networks, while needing less compute to train.
From then on, the Transformer became a "general-purpose architecture": text, images, speech, proteins can all be cut into a string of "words" and handed to it.
Today's GPT, Claude and DeepSeek are all built on it.
Epilogue — Two Ways of "Seeing"
Behind this lies a lesson that runs through the whole history of AI: built-in human knowledge is very useful when data is scarce, but in the face of massive data and compute,
more general methods usually have the last laugh. In 2019, the reinforcement-learning pioneer Richard Sutton wrote this up in a short essay titled The Bitter Lesson. In the next chapter, we'll see what happened once Transformers were scaled up enormously: large language models.
1. Convolution
Slide a small window (the kernel, say 3×3) across the whole image. At each position, multiply the pixels in the window by the matching kernel entries and add them up.
The result is a new "feature map" that says "how strongly this pattern shows up at each position."
Convolution has two key properties:
Local connections: each output looks at only a small patch of the input;
Weight sharing: the same kernel is reused at every position.
Together, these cut the number of parameters by several orders of magnitude, and they also give the network a built-in sense that "the same pattern in a different place is the same thing."
In a CNN the kernels are learned. After training, the first layer's kernels often really do look like edge detectors, just like the cat's simple cells.
First appears in: Prologue convolution.py
2. Receptive fields
The region of the input a neuron can "see" is called its receptive field. A 3×3 convolution sees a 3×3 region; stack two and it's 5×5; the more layers, the bigger the view.
That is why a CNN builds its understanding from local to global, layer by layer: shallow layers see edges, middle layers see textures and parts, deep layers see whole objects.
3. Pooling and translation invariance
Max pooling keeps only the largest value in a small region (say, 2×2).
It only cares whether "this feature shows up somewhere in this patch," not exactly which pixel it's on. Shift the image a little, and the pooled result barely changes. This is called translation invariance.
It is exactly what Fukushima's "C layers" do, corresponding to Hubel and Wiesel's "complex cells."
First appears in: Act 1 convolution.py
4. RNNs and LSTMs
RNN: h_t = tanh(W·h_{t-1} + U·x_t). For each word it reads, it computes a new state from "the old state + the new word."
It is a natural fit for sequences, but at every step the information gets multiplied by a matrix once more, so over long distances it vanishes (or explodes).
An LSTM adds a separate memory cell c, plus three gates:
Forget gate: how much of the old memory to keep;
Input gate: how much new information to write in;
Output gate: how much to output.
Updating the memory cell is mainly "multiply by the forget gate + add new content." When the forget gate is close to 1, information and gradients can both travel a long way almost without loss.
First appears in: Act 3 rnn_vs_attention.py
5. seq2seq and the bottleneck
seq2seq = encoder + decoder: the encoder reads the input sequence into a vector, and the decoder generates the output sequence from that vector.
Translation, summarization and dialogue can all be seen as seq2seq problems.
Its bottleneck: the information of the whole sentence is squeezed into one fixed-length vector, and the longer the sentence, the more gets lost. Attention was born precisely to solve this problem.
First appears in: Act 4
6. Attention (Q, K, V)
You can think of attention as a "fuzzy dictionary lookup":
Query: what I'm looking for;
Key: each candidate's "label";
Value: each candidate's actual content.
How it's computed:
1. Take the dot product of the query with each key to get a "match score";
2. Divide by √d (d is the vector dimension) to keep the scores from getting too big;
3. Use softmax to turn the scores into weights that add up to 1;
4. Use the weights to take a weighted average of all the values.
Unlike a normal dictionary, attention doesn't "take just one"; it "takes a little of each, according to relevance." This makes it differentiable everywhere, so it can be trained with backpropagation.
First appears in: Act 4 attention.py
7. Self-attention and multi-head attention
Self-attention: Q, K and V all come from the same sentence. Every word uses its own query to match against the keys of all the words in the sentence.
That way, "it" can find "kitten" directly, no matter how many words lie in between.
Multi-head attention: use several sets of Q/K/V projections at once (several "heads"). Each head can learn to watch a different relationship:
what a word refers to, grammar, which words sit next to each other... Finally, the heads' results are concatenated.
Causal mask: when generating text, each word may only look at the words before it (the upper-right corner of the attention matrix is masked out);
otherwise the model could "peek at the answer" during training. Models like GPT all use a causal mask.
First appears in: Act 5 attention.py
8. Positional encoding
Attention by itself is "orderless": shuffle the words in a sentence, and the attention result each word gets doesn't change. But "the cat chases the dog" and "the dog chases the cat" mean completely different things.
So each position gets a unique vector added to it (the positional encoding). The 2017 paper combined sine and cosine waves of different frequencies;
today's large models mostly use improved methods such as rotary positional embeddings (RoPE).
First appears in: Act 5 attention.py, transformer_block.py
9. Residual connections and layer normalization
Residual connections: output = input + f(input). Each layer only learns "a change," and the original information has a direct shortcut through.
This solved the vanishing-gradient problem of deep networks, and it also prevents what you saw in Lab 4, where "every word's vector becomes identical" (researchers call this rank collapse).
Layer normalization: adjust each word's vector to mean 0 and variance 1, so the numbers at every layer stay in a stable range and training goes more smoothly.
First appears in: Act 2 transformer_block.py
10. The Transformer block
One Transformer block = multi-head self-attention (words exchange information with each other) + a feed-forward network (each word goes through a two-layer transformation on its own),
with each part wrapped in a residual connection and layer normalization. Stack dozens to a hundred or more of these blocks, and you have the body of a large model.
The parameters live mainly in three places: the word embeddings and output layer, the Q/K/V/O matrices of attention, and the feed-forward networks. The feed-forward networks usually take the largest share.
First appears in: Act 5 transformer_block.py
Cut an image into small patches of a fixed size (say, 16×16 pixels), flatten each patch and treat it as a "word," add positional encoding, and hand it to a standard Transformer.
It shows that the Transformer is a general-purpose sequence processor: anything that can be cut into "a string of words" can be learned with it.
